Users give embodied agents instructions to carry out later, and sometimes they withdraw them. Whether the agent respects the withdrawal is only tested when the cue appears and nobody is asking. We measure what embodied language-model agents do at that moment. In EPM-Bench an agent does chores in AI2-THOR kitchens and Habitat apartments. The user gives it a deferred instruction ("if you notice the toaster is on, turn it off"), the world switches the trigger on out of the agent's view, and in matched counterfactual episodes the user has cancelled the instruction beforehand. Every outcome is read from the state of the simulated world, not from what the agent says. We keep the actor fixed and vary only how its memory stores the instruction. Across six model families, the memory designs in common use (a re-injected briefing, retrieval, a rolling summary) make the agent carry out the cancelled instruction in about half of the episodes, and in about a third even with frontier models. The problem is how the instruction is stored rather than the model itself: if the instruction is stored as an entry with a status that is rewritten when the user cancels, the rate drops below 7% for every model, and live instructions are still executed as often as before. A second model can maintain the entry without access to the ground truth, with 8 to 15% commission, so the design can be used in practice. The design has a limit, however. With four to six standing intentions the cancelled one is still executed in 30 to 35% of episodes even though the correct entry is in view, and at six no design we tested is better than the raw store. This case, and revocations that are implied rather than stated, remain open. We release the suite, the grader and all traces.
